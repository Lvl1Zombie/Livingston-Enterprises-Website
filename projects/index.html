Furniture and door restoration project notes

Three very different assignments illustrate why a useful assessment begins with construction, condition and the owner’s goal—not a predetermined refinishing package.

Project photography is being organized for publication. These initial notes document the known scope without substituting stock imagery for the actual work.

Historic chapel doors: removing eight layers of paint

Doors at the Coleman Chapel on Route 501 north of Lititz had accumulated eight layers of paint. The request was not another coating; it was to return the doors toward a natural wood appearance.

Projects like this reveal their real condition gradually. Profiles, panel edges and old hardware areas trap finish, while previous repairs or changes may remain concealed until stripping begins. Once the old coatings were removed, the exposed wood could be prepared and protected with an exterior finish appropriate for weather exposure.

Why it matters: architectural doors can contain material and workmanship that is difficult to replace. The cost of restoration should be compared with a replacement of genuinely similar construction—not the least expensive modern door.

A handmade antique desk: structure and finish together

A handmade desk more than 90 years old needed restoration rather than a quick surface refresh. Older desks combine moving drawers, casework, hardware and a broad writing surface; failure in one area can affect how the whole piece functions.

The project required attention to both construction and appearance. The aim was to return the desk to use while respecting the age and hand-built qualities its owner valued. That meant planning repair before final color work rather than treating the two as unrelated services.

Why it matters: an heirloom’s value is often a combination of family history, material quality and daily usefulness. Restoration succeeds when those priorities are discussed before work begins.

A century-old rocking chair: stable, but still familiar

The owners of a roughly 100-year-old rocking chair wanted it sturdy enough for another generation without stripping away the originality that made it feel like their chair. The assignment centered on structural preparation for upholstery rather than making every surface appear new.

This is an important restoration distinction: “better” is not always “newer-looking.” A controlled repair can preserve familiar wear, movement and surface character while correcting the weakness that prevents safe use.

Why it matters: the desired endpoint should be stated in plain language. “Preserve its age,” “match the dining room,” and “make it look new” are three different directions.

What future project records will include

Each completed case study should pair original before-and-after photographs with wood species, damage, treatment, finish choice, location, schedule and a customer-approved quotation. This creates a useful record for future customers rather than a gallery of unexplained images.
